# Why Bread Goes Stale in the Fridge

Most people store bread in the refrigerator to keep it fresh. For anything longer than a day, that is the wrong move. Bread staling is not caused by water loss alone; it is mainly a process called starch retrogradation.

Freshly baked bread contains starch granules that have been gelatinized by heat: water has been absorbed into the starch and the granules are swollen and soft. As the bread cools, the amylopectin chains inside those granules begin to re-crystallize, pushing water back out of the starch structure. The crumb turns firm and crumbly even though its total moisture content has barely changed. This is why stale bread feels dry without being dry.

Temperature controls the rate. Retrogradation is fastest between roughly -5°C and 4°C, which is exactly the range a domestic refrigerator holds. At room temperature the process runs several times slower. Below about -18°C it nearly stops, which is why freezing preserves texture far better than chilling.

Freezing works only if it is fast and the bread is wrapped. Slow freezing forms large ice crystals that rupture the gluten network and the cell walls, so the loaf collapses on thawing. A tight wrap matters because the freezer is a very dry environment; unprotected bread loses surface water through sublimation and develops freezer burn.

Reheating partly reverses the process. Above roughly 60°C, retrograded amylopectin re-melts and the crumb softens again, which is why toast and reheated bread taste fresher. This reversal is temporary: once the bread cools, retrogradation resumes and typically runs faster than before, so a reheated loaf stales sooner the second time.

Storing bread with a cut apple or a slice of potato is a common home remedy. It adds moisture to the surrounding air and slows surface drying, but it does not stop retrogradation, so it buys hours rather than days.
